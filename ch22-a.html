The Story
Read carefully. Notice the bold words.
🏛️

The Persian empire was an enormous country ruled by just one man: Cyrus the Great. Cyrus made all the laws for Persia to follow. He decided when the army would march to attack another land, and how much tax the people would pay. He was a good king — but he still expected everyone to obey him without question.

By this point in our story, Cyrus had already built the largest empire the world had ever seen, stretching from Asia Minor all the way to India. Millions of people across many different lands answered to him alone. To most of the ancient world, this seemed like the only sensible way to run such a huge territory.

Across the Aegean Sea, Greece was a completely different kind of country. All the Greeks spoke the same language, dressed the same way, and worshipped the same gods. They all travelled to the Olympic Games together and feasted as one people.

But the Greeks did not all obey a single king. Instead, each Greek city made its own laws, kept its own army, and lived by its own customs. The Greeks were horrified by the very idea of obeying one powerful ruler.

Each Greek city-state valued its independence above almost everything else. If a Persian citizen were asked who ruled their country, they would say one name: the king. But if you asked a Greek the same question, they might laugh — because there was no single answer. Every city ruled itself.

Of all the Greek city-states, two grew larger and more powerful than all the rest: Athens and Sparta. And although both cities were proudly Greek, sharing the same gods and the same customs at festivals, the people who lived in these two cities led two of the most different lives imaginable.

Language Focus
Grammar from the story — learn through meaning
📌 Contrast Linkers — "but" and "while"
We use words like "but" and "while" to show a contrast between two ideas.
Persia had one king, but Greece had many independent cities.
While the Persians obeyed one ruler, the Greeks obeyed their own city laws.
The Greeks shared gods and games, but not a single government.
Tip: "While" at the start of a sentence introduces a contrast, similar to "although." "But" joins two contrasting ideas in the middle of a sentence.
📌 Each + singular verb
We use "each" with a singular verb, even when talking about many individual things.
Each city makes its own laws.
Each Greek city-state has its own army.
Each Persian obeys the same single king.
Tip: Even though "each" refers to many things one at a time, the verb stays singular (has, not have).
📌 "the same...as" for comparison
We use "the same...as" to say two things are identical or equal.
All the Greeks worshipped the same gods as each other.
Sparta was not the same as Athens.
Greece was not organized the same way as Persia.
Tip: Pattern: the same + noun + as. It is a common way to compare similarities and differences.

Ideas & Society
Think about big ideas. There is no wrong answer.
🗽
Independence can be more important than unity. The Greeks refused to unite under one king, even though staying divided may have made them weaker militarily. Why might people choose freedom over strength in numbers?
🌍
Shared culture does not always mean shared government. The Greeks shared a language, gods, and games, but not a government. Can people belong to the same culture while living under very different laws?
⚖️
Different systems suit different values. Persia valued order and obedience; Greece valued freedom and independence. Is one of these values more important than the other, or are both necessary in a society?
🏛️
Bigger is not always more powerful. Even though Persia was a single, massive empire, the small, divided Greek city-states would soon prove surprisingly strong. Why might unity not always guarantee strength?

💡 Big Idea

While the enormous Persian Empire obeyed a single king, the Greek city-states — especially Athens and Sparta — proudly governed themselves, sharing a culture but never a single ruler. People do not need the same government to share the same identity.
