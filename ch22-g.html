The Story
Read carefully. Notice the bold words.
⚡

By now, the enormous contrast between Sparta and Athens should be clear. In Sparta, boys were taken from their families at seven years old and shaped into silent, obedient warriors, while girls trained to become the strong mothers of future soldiers. In Athens, boys studied reading, mathematics, poetry, and music, while girls learned to manage a home — and every male citizen had a genuine voice in how his city was governed.

These two distinct ways of life grew from two very different sets of values. Sparta wanted, above everything else, to be strong and victorious in battle. Athens wanted, above everything else, to be wise, educated, and free to govern itself. And yet both cities were unmistakably Greek — sharing the same gods, the same traditions, and the same fierce love of independence from any single foreign king.

Athens gave the world an enormous legacy: the idea of democracy, and great thinkers like Plato, whose philosophy is still studied today, thousands of years later. Sparta gave the world a different kind of legacy — the very word we still use for someone who endures hardship without complaint.

Together, these two rivals shaped much of what we remember about ancient Greece. Their influence would prove far greater than either city could have imagined at the time — from ideas about government and civic duty, to ideas about discipline, courage, and sacrifice.

But there was very little time left for Sparta and Athens to enjoy their rivalry in peace. Across the Aegean Sea, the enormous Persian Empire — the same empire once built by Cyrus the Great — was growing restless. Greece had refused to bow down and obey a single foreign king. Very soon, Sparta and Athens would need to decide whether their fierce independence was worth defending together, against the largest empire the world had ever known.

Language Focus
Grammar from the story — learn through meaning
📌 Comparatives and Contrast Review
We use comparative structures to highlight differences between two things.
Sparta was more focused on strength than Athens.
Athens was more concerned with education than Sparta.
Sparta’s values were very different from Athens’s values.
Tip: Comparative language ("more...than," "different from") is essential for describing contrasts between people, places, or ideas.
📌 Future in the Past — "would need to"
We use "would" to describe the future as it was seen from a point in the past — common in historical narration.
Sparta and Athens would need to decide whether to work together.
Greece would soon face a great empire from across the sea.
No one yet knew what would happen next.
Tip: This structure describes the future from the perspective of characters living in the past, adding suspense to historical storytelling.
📌 "despite" + noun phrase
We use "despite" to show a contrast, followed directly by a noun or noun phrase (not a full clause).
Despite their differences, both cities were Greek.
Despite the rivalry, they shared the same gods.
Despite Sparta’s toughness, Athens developed a lasting legacy of its own.
Tip: "Despite" is followed by a noun or -ing form, unlike "although," which is followed by a full clause (subject + verb).

Chapter Review

📜 Chapter 22 Summary

In Chapter 22, we explored two of ancient Greece’s greatest city-states: Sparta and Athens. While Persia was ruled by one powerful king, Greece stayed proudly divided into independent city-states, each with its own laws and army. In Sparta, boys were sent to harsh military camps at seven, trained to be brave, obedient, and silent, while girls trained to become strong mothers of soldiers — all captured vividly in the story of the boy and the fox. In Athens, citizens governed themselves through democracy, debating and voting at the forum, while children were educated in reading, mathematics, poetry, and music rather than warfare. The philosopher Plato warned that only educated citizens could protect a democracy from tyranny. Though Sparta and Athens held very different values, both were unmistakably Greek — and by the end of the chapter, the growing power of the Persian Empire across the sea hinted at conflicts still to come.

💡 Big Idea

Sparta and Athens built two of the most different societies in the ancient world — one shaped entirely around military strength, the other around democracy, wisdom, and education. A single culture can produce completely different civilisations, depending on which values it chooses to live by.
