2
The Story
Read carefully. Notice the bold words.
📖

Education in Athens looked completely different from education in Sparta. Spartan boys were trained to be brave, strong, and obedient soldiers. But Athenian boys had to learn how taxes worked, how laws were made, and how their government functioned, since they would grow up to vote as citizens.

Athenian boys did go to school, just like Spartan boys — but they did not learn how to fight. Instead, they were taught how to read and how to write on wax tablets. They studied mathematics, learning how to count, add, and subtract.

Athenian boys also had to memorize long passages of the poetry of Homer, reciting his famous stories from memory in front of their teachers and classmates.

Music was another important part of an Athenian boy’s education. Boys learned to play the flute and the lyre, an ancient Greek instrument shaped a little like a small harp. Just like the Spartans, Athenians were still expected to be physically strong — but instead of military drills, they trained as athletes through wrestling and running races against one another.

By the time an Athenian boy became a man, he could read, write, calculate, recite great poetry, play a musical instrument, and hold his own in a wrestling match — a very different set of skills from the tough, silent soldiers being raised at the very same time, just a short distance away in Sparta.

Teachers in Athens were often hired privately by wealthy families, and lessons could take place in a teacher’s home, in a public building, or even outdoors under the shade of a tree. Learning was taken seriously, but it was also considered something to be enjoyed, unlike the strict, silent discipline demanded inside a Spartan training camp.

Language Focus
Grammar from the story — learn through meaning
📌 "instead of" + noun / -ing
We use "instead of" to show that one thing replaces another.
Instead of military training, Athenian boys studied reading and writing.
They trained as athletes instead of soldiers.
Boys played the lyre instead of practising with weapons.
Tip: After "instead of," we use a noun (soldiers) or an -ing verb (training), never a base verb.
📌 "had to" for past obligation
We use "had to" to describe something that was necessary or required in the past.
Athenian boys had to memorize Homer’s poetry.
They had to learn how taxes and laws worked.
Boys had to practise writing on wax tablets.
Tip: "Had to" is the past form of "must," showing a past obligation or requirement.
📌 "By the time..." for a completed process
We use "by the time" to show that something was true or complete before a certain later point.
By the time he became a man, an Athenian boy could read, write, and recite poetry.
By the time school finished, he had learned mathematics and music.
By the time he was an adult, he understood how the government worked.
Tip: This structure often pairs with the past perfect ("had learned") to show something completed before another past moment.

Ideas & Society
Think about big ideas. There is no wrong answer.
🎨
A society’s art reflects what it values. Athens valued poetry and music as much as physical strength. What do the arts and subjects a society teaches its children reveal about what that society values most?
⚖️
There is more than one way to build a "strong" citizen. Sparta built physically tough citizens; Athens built educated, well-rounded citizens. Which kind of strength do you think matters more for a peaceful, successful society?
📚
Memorizing poetry was once central to education. Today, few students memorize long poems by heart. What might we gain — or lose — by memorizing great literature as the Athenians did?
🌉
Neighbours can develop in completely opposite directions. Athens and Sparta were close neighbours who still grew into extremely different societies. Why do you think neighbouring places sometimes develop such different values and ways of life?

💡 Big Idea

While Spartan boys trained only for war, Athenian boys grew up learning reading, writing, mathematics, poetry, and music, alongside physical training in wrestling and racing. A city’s idea of a "good education" reveals what kind of citizens — and what kind of future — it hopes to build.
