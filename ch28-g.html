The Story
Read carefully. Notice the bold words.
🏟️

🏋️ At the gladiator school, Servius slept in a small cell, like a prison. Every day he was taken out to train. First, two trainers tested him. They were big men with scars on their faces. They poked him, squeezed his arms, and made him run to a post and back. "Very good," one said. "Now let's test your endurance." All day, Servius had to run, jump, wrestle, and climb. By night he was covered in sweat and mud. But he had passed the test.

🗣️ The next day, Servius stood with five other new prisoners. "Repeat the oath of the gladiator!" a trainer shouted. The oath was a terrible promise: to be burned, to be chained, to be beaten, and to die by the sword. "To be a gladiator is a wonderful privilege!" the trainer said. Servius did not feel lucky at all. But everyone else was repeating the oath, so he did too.

🪵 Training began with wooden swords and straw men. Then the students fought each other, still with wooden swords. Once, Servius did not parry quickly enough, and another student's sword crashed against his side. He was sore for days. The trainers only laughed. "You'd better learn to be tough," they said.

⛑️ Soon Servius learned that he would be a secutor — a gladiator who chased net-fighters. He had a short sword, a large shield, a metal leg-guard, and a round helmet with two tiny eye-holes. He could hardly see!

🏟️ On the day of the fight, Servius was pushed into the arena. Wooden seats all around were full of men, women, and even children. They were cheering. The helmet was hot and tight. A net-fighter crept towards him and threw his net, but it hit Servius's shield. Servius moved forward. Suddenly the net-fighter turned and ran! "He's as frightened as I am!" Servius thought. His heavy armor slowed him down, but then the net-fighter tripped and fell.

👎 Servius had won! He looked up at the crowd. They were booing and making the thumbs-down sign. That meant: "Kill him!" If the crowd liked a fighter, they might turn their thumbs up, and the winner could show mercy.

"Get up. I can't kill you. I am not an animal! It would be wrong to kill a man for sport."

🕊️ Servius put his sword away. The net-fighter ran off, hardly able to believe it. The crowd booed louder, but Servius walked back to his trainer. He knew he might be punished, but he could not kill another man.

📜 The Romans were great and powerful, but they were also bloodthirsty. Some Roman writers thought this was wrong. The thinker Seneca wrote that these brave refusals taught the crowd a lesson: it is more decent to die than to kill.

Language Focus
Grammar from the story — learn through meaning
📌 Second/third conditional idea: 'If ... had ..., ... might have ...'
Use 'if + had + past participle' to imagine a different past.
If the net-fighter had been brave, the crowd might have had pity.
If Servius had parried faster, he would not have been hurt.
If he had killed the man, the crowd would have cheered.
💡 Tip: This talks about things that did NOT happen.
📌 Reported commands: 'force/make someone do'
Use force + someone + to + verb, or make + someone + verb.
The trainers forced Servius to run, jump, and climb.
They made him repeat the oath.
The crowd wanted to make him kill his opponent.
💡 Tip: After 'make', do not use 'to'. After 'force', use 'to'.
📌 'as ... as' comparisons
Use as + adjective + as to say two things are the same.
He's as frightened as I am!
The helmet was as hot as an oven.
The concrete dried as hard as stone.
💡 Tip: 'Not as ... as' shows that two things are different.

Ideas & Society
Think about big ideas. There is no wrong answer.
👥
Following the crowd. Everyone repeated the oath, so Servius did too. Later, he refused to obey the crowd. When is it right to say no to a group?
🕊️
Mercy and strength. Servius was strong enough to win, but he chose mercy. Is mercy a kind of strength?
🎟️
Violence as entertainment. Roman families cheered at fights. Why do people sometimes enjoy watching violence in films or games?
📜
Speaking up. Seneca wrote that the games were wrong, even though many Romans loved them. Why is it important for some people to question their own society?

📖 Chapter 28 Summary

The Romans borrowed the gods of Greece from the Etruscans and gave them Roman names: Zeus became Jupiter, Poseidon became Neptune, and the god of war was Mars. They told myths to explain nature, like the story of Ceres and her daughter Proserpine, who was taken to the underworld by Pluto. Because Proserpine ate six pomegranate seeds, she stays below for six months each year — and that is why we have winter and summer. Rome conquered all of Italy and built strong roads, like the Appian Way, with mile stones to show distances. The Romans were the first to use concrete, built tall apartment buildings, and carried water to their cities on aqueducts for drinking and for the public baths. But Rome was also bloodthirsty. Prisoners like the imaginary Servius were forced to train as gladiators and fight in the arena while crowds cheered. When the crowd told Servius to kill his opponent, he showed mercy — and some Romans, like Seneca, agreed that it is more decent to die than to kill.

💡 The Big Idea of Chapter 28

Rome was brilliant at borrowing ideas and building great things — roads, concrete, aqueducts. But a truly great society is measured not only by what it builds, but by how it treats people.
