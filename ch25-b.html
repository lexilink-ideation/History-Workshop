2
The Story
Read carefully. Notice the bold words.
⚔️

Alexander had many opportunities to test his courage on the battlefield, riding his beloved warhorse Bucephalus into battle after battle. His father Philip had only conquered Greece; Alexander wanted the mighty Persian Empire itself, which stretched all the way from Asia Minor to distant India.

When Alexander met the Persian army in Asia Minor, he cleverly used his cavalry to win a decisive victory. According to legend, he then stopped at a city where a chariot was tied to its axle with an enormous, tangled knot. "According to prophecy," the locals explained, "whoever loosens that knot will rule all of Asia. But it is completely impossible — hundreds have tried and failed!" Alexander studied the knot, then simply drew his sword and sliced it in half. "There," he said. "I have loosened the knot."

Alexander went on to conquer the rest of Asia, marching into Egypt, where he was crowned pharaoh, before returning to take the remaining Persian territory. Now Alexander wanted to keep going, so his army began to invade India. There, he learned how to use war elephants in combat, and his soldiers won most of their battles — but the Indian warriors were just as fierce as any army he had faced.

After a brutal battle in which over a thousand soldiers were killed or wounded, Alexander's exhausted army staged a mutiny. "Be content with what you have!" they told him. "We don't want to keep dying to make your empire bigger." Alexander sulked in his tent, but finally agreed to stop and turn his energy toward running the huge kingdom he already had.

Wanting the world to remember him, Alexander founded new cities across his empire, naming many of them Alexandria. The most famous Alexandria, in Egypt, grew into the greatest city in the world after his death, home to countless scholars and writers.

Just outside its harbour stood the largest lighthouse the world had ever seen — the Pharos, 330 feet tall, guiding ships safely into port. It became the third of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World, alongside the Hanging Gardens of Babylon and the Great Pyramid. Though the Pharos was destroyed long ago, divers recently found huge stone chunks in the harbour that may be all that remains of this legendary structure.

Language Focus
Grammar from the story — learn through meaning
📌 'According to legend...' — Introducing Uncertain History
We use 'according to legend/tradition/prophecy' to introduce stories that may not be completely factual.
According to legend, Alexander sliced the Gordian Knot with his sword.
According to prophecy, whoever untied the knot would rule all of Asia.
According to some historians, this story may be exaggerated.
Tip: This phrase is essential in historical writing, showing that a story comes from legend rather than confirmed fact.
📌 'Even though' — Unexpected Contrast
We use 'even though' to introduce a surprising or unexpected contrast within a sentence.
Even though his soldiers won many battles, they kept losing more men.
Even though Alexander wanted to continue, his army refused.
Even though he was the greatest conqueror, even Alexander had limits.
Tip: 'Even though' is followed by a full clause (subject + verb), unlike 'despite,' which is followed by a noun.
📌 Purpose Clauses — 'so that'
We use 'so that' to explain the purpose or reason behind an action.
Alexandria was built so that merchants could reach it easily by ship.
The Pharos was built so that ships could sail safely into the harbour.
Alexander founded cities so that future generations would remember him.
Tip: 'So that' is followed by a full clause (subject + verb) and explains WHY something was done.

Ideas & Society
Think about big ideas. There is no wrong answer.
⚔️
Sometimes breaking the rules of a problem is the real solution. Everyone assumed the knot had to be untied by hand, but Alexander redefined the challenge entirely. Can you think of examples where 'thinking outside the box' solved a seemingly impossible problem?
🛑
Even the greatest leaders eventually meet their limits. Alexander, the most successful conqueror in ancient history, was finally stopped not by an enemy, but by his own exhausted soldiers. Why do you think even the most powerful people eventually face limits?
🏙️
Cities can outlive the people who founded them. Alexandria still exists today, thousands of years after Alexander's death, while his empire itself did not survive nearly as long. Why do you think cities often outlast empires and even their founders?
🗼
Great structures can become legendary even after they disappear. The Pharos Lighthouse no longer exists, yet it remains famous as one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World. Why do you think some destroyed or lost structures still capture people's imagination centuries later?

💡 Big Idea

Alexander conquered Asia Minor with his cavalry, boldly sliced through the 'impossible' Gordian Knot, pushed into India until his own army mutinied, and founded Alexandria — home to the legendary Pharos Lighthouse. Bold, unconventional thinking can solve problems that traditional methods cannot, but even the greatest conqueror must eventually recognise his limits.
