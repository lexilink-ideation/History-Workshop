The Story
Read carefully. Notice the bold words.
👑

Cyrus was now the king of both the Medes and the Persians. He was a great warrior, but he was also known as a good and fair king. Even though he had conquered the Medes in battle, he let the Median people continue living peacefully in their own homes. He even allowed Median noblemen to hold some power in his new, combined empire.

Cyrus’s empire had grown so large that he could not possibly manage everything alone. He could not collect every tax, judge every court case, and settle every argument by himself! So he appointed both Persians and Medians as officials across his kingdom. The Medians felt they were being treated with respect, so they had little reason to rebel against Cyrus’s rule.

Now Cyrus decided it was time to make his empire even bigger. He set his sights on Asia Minor, a rich land ruled by King Croesus — the most wealthy king in the entire world, with more gold than anyone else alive. Cyrus knew that if he could conquer Croesus, all that treasure would become his own. So he marched his army to Croesus’s kingdom and conquered it completely.

Cyrus captured Croesus and made him stand on the walls of his own city, forced to watch as Persian soldiers began to loot it, carrying away armloads of gold coins and jewelry. Yet Croesus watched everything calmly, without showing any anger at all.

"How can you stay so calm?" Cyrus finally asked him. "They are robbing you of all your gold!" "No, they are not," Croesus answered. "This city belongs to you now. So really, they are stealing from you."

When Cyrus heard this clever answer, he immediately stopped his soldiers and ordered all the gold to be returned. From that day on, Cyrus continued marching his army eastward, all the way to the Indus River, ruling over an empire that stretched as wide as it was tall.

Language Focus
Grammar from the story — learn through meaning
📌 "even though" for contrast
We use "even though" to introduce a surprising or contrasting fact.
Even though he had conquered the Medes, Cyrus let them stay in their homes.
Even though his empire was huge, he shared power with others.
Even though he was rich, Croesus stayed calm and clever.
Tip: "Even though" is followed by a full clause (subject + verb), unlike "despite," which is followed by a noun.
📌 Modal Verb "could not" for impossibility
We use "could not" to describe something that was impossible to do.
Cyrus could not collect all the taxes himself.
He could not judge every court case alone.
One person could not manage such a huge empire without help.
Tip: "Could not" + base verb shows a past inability, often explaining why a decision or change was necessary.
📌 Direct Speech in Dialogue
We use direct speech, with quotation marks, to show a conversation exactly as it happened.
"How can you be so calm?" Cyrus asked.
"They are actually stealing from you," Croesus replied.
"Take all the gold back," Cyrus ordered.
Tip: Each new speaker usually begins on a new line in written dialogue, though this rule is sometimes relaxed in simple texts.

Ideas & Society
Think about big ideas. There is no wrong answer.
🤝
Sharing power can make an empire stronger. Cyrus let Medians hold official positions instead of ruling everything alone. Why might sharing power make a leader more successful, not less?
💡
A clever answer can change someone’s mind faster than an argument. Croesus did not argue or beg — he simply pointed out a clever truth. Why do you think clever, calm words are sometimes more powerful than anger?
💰
True wealth is not only measured in gold. Croesus lost his kingdom but kept his composure and his cleverness. What do you think matters more: material wealth, or wisdom and character?
🌍
Good treatment of conquered people can prevent future rebellion. Because Cyrus treated the Medians fairly, they did not rebel. What does this suggest about how leaders should treat people they have power over?

💡 Big Idea

Cyrus built a huge empire not only through conquest, but through fairness — sharing power with conquered peoples and listening to wise advice, even from a defeated king. A truly great leader knows that fairness can be just as powerful as force.
