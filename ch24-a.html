The Story
Read carefully. Notice the bold words.
👑

Athens and Sparta did not have very much in common — except for their shared language and their shared gods. In fact, for years, the two cities had spent most of their energy fighting each other, bitter rivals through and through.

But then something far more frightening happened. The Persians — who had already conquered almost every other land around them — began to invade Greece. At first, Persia simply sent messengers, announcing: "He wants you to become part of his empire. Send him some earth and water. Then he will not attack you." The Athenians and Spartans were furious. Instead of agreeing to surrender, they threw the messengers down a well.

After this insult, Persia's empire was determined to attack. Facing this enormous threat, Athens and Sparta finally agreed to stop their quarrel and become allies. The war against Persia began around 500 BC and dragged on for years.

One of the most famous battles of this war was the Battle of Marathon, in 490 BC. The Athenians sent a message to Sparta, begging for help, but the Spartans were in the middle of a religious festival and refused to leave. Badly outnumbered, the Athenians marched to Marathon alone. When the Persians launched arrows at them, the Athenians charged straight through and won, forcing the Persians to retreat.

To share the news, a runner named Pheidippides ran over twenty-six miles to Athens without stopping. He gasped "We have won!" and then — according to legend — died of exhaustion. Today we still run a race called "a marathon" in his honour.

The war continued for years until one final, decisive naval battle at a place called Salamis in 480 BC. The Greeks used clever strategy, luring the much larger Persian fleet into narrow waters where their size became a disadvantage. After this crushing defeat, the Persians finally gave up attacking Greece for good, and the Greek city-states remained free and independent.

Language Focus
Grammar from the story — learn through meaning
📌 Past Perfect — 'had already conquered'
We use the past perfect (had + past participle) to show an action that happened before another past action.
Persia had already conquered almost all the land around them.
By the time the messengers arrived, Persia had already decided to attack.
The two cities had fought each other for years before the Persian threat appeared.
Tip: Past perfect shows which past event happened FIRST, before another past event.
📌 'So...that' — Extreme Results
We use 'so + adjective + that' to describe an extreme result.
The Persians were so startled that they became disorganised.
Pheidippides was so exhausted that he died after delivering the news.
The Athenians were so outnumbered that few believed they could win.
Tip: This structure links a strong quality (so startled) with a dramatic consequence (that they lost).
📌 'Thanks to' — Giving Credit
We use 'thanks to + noun' to explain what caused a positive result.
Thanks to clever strategy, the Greeks defeated a much larger fleet.
Thanks to the courage shown at Marathon, Greece survived the invasion.
Thanks to this victory, Greek culture continued to flourish.
Tip: 'Thanks to' introduces the reason for something good; use 'because of' for neutral or negative results.

Ideas & Society
Think about big ideas. There is no wrong answer.
🤝
A common enemy can unite bitter rivals. Athens and Sparta had fought each other for years, but a Persian threat brought them together. Can you think of a modern example where rivals united against a common threat?
🏃
Physical endurance has always inspired admiration. Pheidippides's legendary run inspired a sport still practised worldwide today. Why do you think physical feats of endurance capture people's imagination so strongly?
🧠
Clever strategy can overcome greater numbers. The smaller Greek fleet defeated a much larger Persian fleet through clever strategy alone. Can you think of other examples in history or sports where strategy beat raw size or strength?
🌍
Empires often expect smaller nations to submit without question. Persia expected Greece to surrender without even fighting. Why do you think large empires often assume smaller nations will simply obey?

💡 Big Idea

Facing the enormous Persian Empire, bitter rivals Athens and Sparta set aside years of conflict, won a legendary victory at Marathon, and used clever strategy to triumph at Salamis. Even the fiercest rivals can unite when survival itself is at stake — and clever strategy can triumph over even the greatest numbers.
